## Abstract 

Large language models (LLMs) have become pivotal in artificial intelligence, 1

demonstrating strong capabilities in reasoning, understanding, and generating 2

data. However, their deployment on edge devices is hindered by their substantial 3

size, often reaching several billion parameters. Quantization is a widely used 4

method to reduce memory usage and inference time, however LLMs present unique 5

challenges due to the prevalence of outliers in their activations. In this work, we 6

leverage the theoretical advantages of Hadamard matrices over random rotation 7

matrices to push the boundaries of quantization in LLMs. We demonstrate that 8

Hadamard matrices are more effective in reducing outliers, which are a significant 9

obstacle in achieving low-bit quantization. Our method based on a gradual binary 10 

search enables 3-bit quantization for weights, activations, and key-value (KV) 11 

caches, resulting in a 40% increase in accuracy on common benchmarks compared 12 

to SoTA methods. We extend the use of rotation matrices to support non-power-13 

of-2 embedding dimensions, similar to the Qwen architecture, by employing the 14 

Paley’s algorithm. Our experimental results on multiple models family like Mistral, 15 

LLaMA, and Qwen demonstrate the effectiveness of our approach, outperforming 16 

existing methods and enabling practical 3-bit quantization. 17 

## 1 Introduction 18 

Large Language Models (LLMs) have become a central component of artificial intelligence due 19 

to their strong capabilities in reasoning, understanding, and generating data. These impressive 20 

capabilities are attributed to the quality of the data used during training, the model architecture, and 21 

the size of the model, which often reaches several billion parameters. This size limitation restricts 22 

their deployment on edge devices. Quantization is a widely used method to reduce memory usage 23 

and inference time [ 12 , 13 ], but the challenges differ compared to those faced with Convolutional 24 

Neural Networks (CNNs) [9, 29]. 25 

Weights are relatively easy to quantize for both CNNs and LLMs and can often achieve ternary 26

known to produce spikes in its layers and for some tokens that can be handled separately or diffused 30 

in the tensor [7, 29]. 31 

One very promising approach to overcome this limitation is to use rotation matrices to redistribute 32 

weights and activation values, thereby minimizing the impact of outliers [ 19 , 2]. Additionally, 33 

methods such as prefix tokens have shown very interesting results in managing outliers in LLMs 34

2.1 Quantization 49 

Quantizing models involves reducing the number of bits required to store and compute model 50 

activations. This process is crucial for deploying LLMs on resource-constrained devices. To achieve 51 

this, we define a scaling factor that determines the distance between quantization bins and the range 52 

of values to be compressed. 53

Such quantization can be applied per-token, where each token has a different scaling factor, or per-56

statistics computed on a subset of the dataset, or dynamic, recomputed at each step. 60 

Quantization can lead to a significant drop in performance when applied post-training (PTQ) [ 30 ]. 61 

To mitigate this, some methods adapt weights to the noise introduced during a training phase (QAT) 62 

[ 18 , 8]. Typically, for LLMs, only linear layers are quantized, as they account for most of the 63 

computation cost, while normalization layers, matrix multiplications, and the softmax function within 64 

the attention block are left unquantized. 65 

2.2 Outliers 66 

Quantizing LLM weights is relatively straightforward and does not require extensive efforts to achieve. 67 

Techniques like GPTQ [ 11 ] enables 8-bit quantization without retraining, preserving model accuracy. 68 

Some QAT methods can even push the boundaries to 1-bit quantization, as seen in BitNet [ 26 ] or 69 

ternary quantization [20]. 70 

However, LLMs present unique challenges due to the prevalence of extreme high values in their 71 

activations [ 27 , 23 , 15 , 17 ]. The scaling factor, which is directly tied to the maximum absolute value, 72 

often causes most of the distribution to be rounded to zero, leading to performance degradation. To 73 

address this, techniques like LLM.int8() [ 7 ] cluster these outliers and quantize them separately from 74 

the main distribution. 75 

Alternative methods, such as SmoothQuant [ 29 ], shift the quantization challenge from activations 76 

to weights by introducing a scaling parameter between them. Other approaches attempt to relocate 77 

these spikes into "sink tokens" before quantization [ 24 ]. Some research focuses on understanding the 78 

upstream causes of these spikes during the learning process to limit their impact post-training [ 23 ]. 79

2.3 Rotation Matrices 82 

2.3.1 Random orthogonal matrices 83 

Rotation matrices play a pivotal role in various applications, including signal processing, computer 84 

vision, and machine learning. These matrices are orthogonal and invertible by their transpose, meaning 85 

they preserve the length of vectors and the angles between them. In the context of quantization, 86 

rotation matrices can be used to decorrelate and redistribute the energy of model activations [ 1 , 2 , 3 ], 87 

making them more amenable to quantization. The idea is to apply orthogonal matrices before 88 

quantization to flatten the distribution and then recover the tensor by applying its inverse (see Figure 89 

1). Part of this process can be pre-computed and fused with weights and the rest needs to be done at 90 

inference [1]. 91 

However, the effectiveness of rotation matrices depends on the specific matrix used. Randomly drawn 92 

orthogonal rotation matrices can introduce noise and reduce the overall performance of the model. 93 

To mitigate this, some methods adapt the rotation matrices during a training phase to better align with 94 

the model’s weights and activations [19]. 95 

In practice, rotation matrices are often used in conjunction with other quantization techniques, such as 96 

GPTQ. This combination allows for more robust and efficient quantization of large language models, 97 

enabling their deployment on resource-constrained devices. 98 

2.3.2 Hadamard matrices 99 

Hadamard matrices are another powerful tool in the quantization arsenal. These matrices are 100 

orthogonal matrices and all their entries are either +1 or -1 making them very efficient to compute 101 

(eq 1). Hadamard matrices have been extensively used in signal processing, error-correcting codes 102 

[14], and more recently, in the quantization of neural networks [1, 2]. 103 

One of the key advantages of Hadamard matrices is their ability to decorrelate the activations of a 104 

model. By applying a Hadamard matrix, the activations are transformed into a new basis where the 105 

correlations between different dimensions are minimized. This decorrelation property is particularly 106 

useful in reducing the impact of outliers, as the extreme values are spread out across multiple 107 

dimensions. 108

## 3 Analysis and theoretical demonstrations 134 

3.1 Clipping Ratio 135 

To perform quantization we can play on several parameters to improve the effectiveness of the process, 136 

for example in LSQ [ 9] they optimise the scaling factor trough training, or FracBits [ 31 ] which tries 137 

to find the best precision for every layer. Other works highlighted the importance of the clipping ratio 138 

like PACT [ 5] where the optimization is done during training. Some others apply a Grid Search [ 4] to 139 

find the best configuration particularly useful for LLMs where training or fine tuning can be very 140 

time consuming. 141 

Clipping ratios are essential for managing outliers, as they establish the balance between maintaining 142 

high precision for small values and preserving a maximum value close to its original. However, the 143 

model exhibits significant variability in how quantization responds to changes in the clipping ratio. 144 

While some projections can tolerate very low clipping ratios, others experience a substantial accuracy 145 

drop with even slight adjustments (see Appendix C). Therefore, to effectively manage this variability, 146 

a tailored clipping ratio must be determined for each projection. 147 

Previous studies have shown that quantization error is not always the best metric to guide the opti-148 

mization process for quantization parameters [ 21 ]. Specifically, at very low precision levels, such as 4 149 

or 3 bits, the set of quantized weights deviates significantly from the optimized configuration obtained 150 

during training. Attempting to recover this configuration using quantization error often results in 151 

an ineffective set of weights. To address this issue, we can use perplexity as an objective function. 152 

4Perplexity provides a more accurate representation of model performance and is computationally 153 

efficient, as it is based on Cross Entropy Loss, which is frequently used during training for its 154 

smoothness. 155

## 4 Method 174 

4.1 Gradual Binary Search 175 

In Section 3.1, we emphasize the importance of the clipping ratio parameter and its significant impact 176 

on model performance. We stress the need to optimize each projection with its own clipping ratio 177 

for best results. Our primary contribution is an algorithm that determines the optimal clipping ratio 178 

for each quantizer using a binary search (Algorithm 1). To drive the binary search, we minimize 179 

perplexity across various clipping ratios, assuming a single minimum and a convex landscape. 180 

Additionally, we quantize our model gradually: first, we quantize and optimize the initial linear 181 

projection while keeping the rest in FP16, then use the obtained parameters to quantize and optimize 182 

the next projection, and so on. This process is discussed in Appendix C where we experimentally 183 

show the necessity to optimize gradually the clipping ratio. 184

## 6 Conclusion 263 

In this work, we introduced an approach to optimize the quantization of LLMs using Gradual 264 

Binary Search and Hadamard matrices. Our method achieves efficient 3-bit quantization for weights, 265 

activations, and key-value caches, significantly improving model performance. We also theoretically 266 

demonstrated that Hadamard matrices are more effective than random rotation matrices in reducing 267 

extreme values in activations. 268 

We also extended the use of rotation matrices to support non-power-of-2 embedding dimensions 269 

using the Paley algorithm and dimension expansion. This generalization allows our method to be 270 

applied to various architectures, including those with unique embedding dimensions. Experimental 271 

results on models from the Mistral library, LLaMA architecture, and Qwen show the effectiveness of 272 

our approach, outperforming existing methods. 273 

Overall, our findings suggest that GBS and Hadamard matrices have great potential for advancing 274 

LLM quantization, making them more suitable for resource-constrained devices. Future work will 275 

explore mix computation and combining GBS with other methods. 276 

## 7 Limitations 277 

As explained in the previous part expanding dimensions has a big computational cost and it worsen 278 

with context length that is why we need to be aware of the expanding limit. One potential solution is 279 

to implement a Mixed Computation pipeline, where dimensions are only expanded in specific layers 280 

based on the presence of outliers, thereby substantially reducing computational overhead. 281 

Another challenge arises with GBS, which involves computing perplexity at each step—a process 282 

that can be time-consuming for PTQ methods, sometimes taking several days. To mitigate this, we 283 

could use less than 10% of WikiText2 which might be excessive and unnecessary. 284

Guidelines: 481 

• The answer NA means that the paper has no limitation while the answer No means that 482

• The paper should point out any strong assumptions and how robust the results are to 485 

violations of these assumptions (e.g., independence assumptions, noiseless settings, 486

limitations that aren’t acknowledged in the paper. The authors should use their best 504
